# Under the Wave off Kanagawa — "The Great Wave" (c. 1830–32) — verified research

Print: Katsushika Hokusai, *Kanagawa oki nami ura*, from *Thirty-six Views of Mount
Fuji* (*Fugaku sanjūrokkei*), c. 1830–32. Polychrome woodblock print; impressions in
many collections (The Met's is among the earliest printed).
Room: **Awe / Impermanence**. Storytelling mode: **environmental / historical only** —
no quotes are used in this experience.

This file is the gate (CLAUDE.md rule 10): nothing ships that isn't cleared here.

---

## Verified facts

| # | Fact | Source |
|---|---|---|
| F1 | Made c. 1830–32, when Hokusai was in his seventies, as part of the *Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji*, published by Nishimura Yohachi. | The Met, catalog entry: https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/45434 ; Met essay "The Great Wave: Anatomy of an Icon": https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/hokusai-great-wave |
| F2 | It is a woodblock **print**, produced in many impressions — reproducible art, affordably sold, not a single canvas. | The Met (as F1) |
| F3 | Its deep blue is **Prussian blue**, a pigment then newly imported to Japan and advertised as a novelty by the print's publisher. | The Met (as F1) |
| F4 | Three boats move through the storm-tossed sea beneath the cresting wave. | The Met (as F1) |
| F5 | Mount Fuji appears as a small, stable triangle in the wave's hollow — perspective deliberately making Japan's grandest mountain tiny and still. | The Met (as F1) |

## Cleared interpretive lines (environmental, fact-grounded)

- "The mountain does not move. Everything else is one breath from change." (F5)
- "Fast boats ferrying the morning's catch, rowers bent to the sea." (F4; the vessels
  are commonly identified as oshiokuri-bune, fish-transport boats — The Met essay)
- "Blue from Prussia, new to Japan that decade." (F3)
- "Not one painting: thousands of impressions, made to be owned by anyone." (F2)

## Not cleared — do not ship

- The claim that a print cost "the same as a bowl of noodles" — often repeated,
  not verified here.
- Any inner monologue for Hokusai; any reading of the wave as menace or disaster
  (the print is motion and awe, not catastrophe).

## Emotional-ceiling guidance

Impermanence here is serene, cyclical, almost amused: the wave is always about to fall
and never falls; Fuji outlasts it. Close on stillness (Fuji), never on threat.
