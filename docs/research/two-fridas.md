## Verified facts

| # | Fact | Source |
|---|---|---|
| F1 | Painted in 1939, the year of Kahlo's divorce from Diego Rivera. | Britannica: https://www.britannica.com/topic/The-Two-Fridas |

| F3 | Both hearts are exposed and joined by a vein; the white-dressed Frida's heart is torn open, and she holds surgical pincers over the cut vein; the Tehuana Frida holds a small portrait of Diego Rivera as a child. | Britannica (as F1) |

## Authentic diary passages (cleared, with caveat)

**Caveat:** the diary is in Spanish; English renderings vary between editions. Reviewer:
check against the published 1995 translation before shipping. Any elision must show an
ellipsis; never splice.

### K1 — the origin (vein node)
> "I must have been 6 years old when I experienced intensely an imaginary friendship
> with a little girl more or less the same age as me."

- *The Diary of Frida Kahlo* (1995) — the entry Kahlo herself connected to the painting's
  origin ("Origin of The Two Fridas", 1939).

### K2 — the friend (held-hands node)
> "I do not remember her image or her color. But I do know that she laughed a lot
> without sounds. She was agile and she danced as if she weighed nothing at all."

- Same diary entry and caveat as K1.

## Cleared interpretive lines (environmental, fact-grounded)

- "Painted in the year of their divorce; one heart open to the storm." (F1, F3)
- "In her hand, a tiny portrait: Diego, as a child." (F3)

## Not cleared — do not ship

- The oft-quoted "I suffered two grave accidents in my life… the other accident is
  Diego" — real Kahlo remark but not verified here; do not use without sourcing.
- Any claim that Kahlo *stated* the two figures are "the Frida Diego loved and the one
  he no longer loved" — circulates widely, unverified here.

## Emotional-ceiling guidance (rule 15)

The torn heart, the pincers, the divorce are all present — as weather, not verdict. The
painting's own gesture is the answer: the hands stay joined. Connection and Return must
center the held hands and the companion who never left; the visitor leaves with
endurance, not wound.
